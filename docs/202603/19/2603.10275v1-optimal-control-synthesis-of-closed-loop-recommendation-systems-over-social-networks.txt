Abstract: This paper addresses the problem of designing recommendation systems for social networks and e-commerce platforms from a control-theoretic perspective. We treat the design of recommendation systems as a state-feedback infinite-horizon optimal control problem with a performance index that (i) rewards alignment/engagement, (ii) penalizes polarization and large deviations from an uncontrolled baseline, and (iii) regularizes exposure across neighboring users. The recommendation entries are fed to the platform users, who are assumed to follow a networked, multi-topic, continuous-time opinion dynamics. We show that the designed control yields a stabilizing recommendation system under simple algebraic/spectral conditions on the weights that encode the platform’s preference for engagement, stability of preferences, polarization, and cross-user diversity. Conversely, we show that when ill-posed weights are selected in the optimal control problem (namely, when engagement is excessively rewarded), the closed-loop system can exhibit destabilizing, pathological behaviors that conflict with the design objectives. 

Keywords: Social networks and opinion dynamics; Control of networks; Applications of optimal control; Recommendation systems; 1. INTRODUCTION In social networking and e-commerce platforms, person-alized suggestions that optimize short-term engagement interact with the confirmation bias of the users and can produce various “information disorders”, such as extreme, polarizing opinions and echo chambers (Gausen et al., 2022; Husz´ ar et al., 2022; Bail et al., 2018). Previous work has suggested that recommendation systems, when tuned purely for engagement, steer users toward extreme positions, and that extreme positions, in turn, increase en-gagement. In the single-user model by Rossi et al. (2021), this mechanism is formalized in a closed loop, providing both a baseline mechanism and a benchmark phenomenon. Related theory confirms that, without sustained explo-ration, positions can drift to extremes (Jiang et al., 2019), and repeated learning on self-influenced logs can reduce diversity and long-run utility (Chaney et al., 2018). Despite evidence of unwanted effects, mitigation is typi-cally addressed only after deployment. The recent survey by Dean et al. (2025) argues that most recommendation pipelines are designed without an explicit model of how users and algorithms shape one another and highlights several structural issues: memoryless architectures (Cov-ington et al., 2016); simplified or omitted user dynamics and creator adaptation; and optimization aggressively ori-

ented toward engagement (Chen et al., 2019; Immorlica et al., 2024). Mitigation is then performed a posteriori on logged data, which might obscure causal effects and favor myopic, symptom-level fixes (Sinha et al., 2016; Chaney et al., 2018), in turn degrading platform performance even more and inducing user- and society-level harms, including shifts in exposure composition, reduced content diversity, and polarization (Mansoury et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2014). In this paper, we aim to overcome these limitations by treating the design of recommendation systems as an infinite-horizon optimal control problem posed at the level of user–platform interaction. We begin from a performance index that makes explicit the design trade-offs, since it (i) rewards alignment between recommendations and user opinions as a proxy for engagement, (ii) penalizes polarization and large deviations from an uncontrolled baseline, and (iii) regularizes exposure across neighboring users in the social graph. These terms define a family of weights that encode the platform’s normative choices about engagement, stability of preferences, polarization, and cross-user diversity. The recommendations then enter, as control input, acontinuous-time opinion dynamics model that encom-passes key aspects of the platform’s behavior. Our anal-ysis adopts a multi-topic opinion-formation model, akin to Friedkin et al. (2016), in the continuous-time version

proposed by Ye et al. (2020). Social interactions are rep-resented by a graph Laplacian, the inter-topic logic is captured by a suitable coupling matrix, and an anchoring term models the retention of the inner beliefs. Our analysis is model-based and yields explicit algebraic and spectral conditions under which the closed-loop rec-ommendation–user system is stable and admits a unique equilibrium with a transparent dependence on the design weights. Conversely, when ill-posed weights are selected, e.g., when engagement is overemphasized relative to reg-ularization and polarization penalties, we prove that the same closed-loop architecture can exhibit pathological be-haviors that conflict with the design objectives, such as unbounded growth of opinions or amplification of disagree-ment, or even the absence of an optimal recommendation input. In this way, the control-theoretic viewpoint both clarifies when recommendation objectives are well-posed and provides tools to design interaction mechanisms with provable guarantees, upstream of deployment and in line with the interaction-focused agenda of Dean et al. (2025). The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the performance index and formu-late the infinite-horizon optimal control problem used to generate the recommendation inputs. Section 3 presents the model of the opinion dynamics of the users. In Sec-tion 4, we analyze the resulting LQ problem and charac-terize how the selection of the weights in the performance index might affect how to solve the LQ problem. Section 5 highlights the pathological behaviors that can arise when the design weights in the performance index are chosen inappropriately. Finally, Section 6 discusses the modeling and design choices, summarizes the main findings and their limitations, and outlines possible extensions.

In this setting, we interpret a free–endpoint formulation as one where the asymptotic state lim t→∞ x(t) is not explicitly constrained, and a fixed–endpoint formulation as one where the closed-loop state is required to converge to a prescribed equilib-rium (e.g., the uncontrolled baseline xeq or the origin). In this context, choosing a stabilizing feedback corresponds to ensuring that the closed-loop dynamics are asymptotically stable, so that trajectories converge to this equilibrium. When eℓsq (x, v ) is strictly positive definite, the cost is strictly convex and bounded below, and we are in the clas-sical infinite-horizon LQ problem (Anderson and Moore, 2007; Trentelman et al., 2002). In this case, the algebraic Riccati equation admits a unique stabilizing solution, the optimal recommendation policy is a stabilizing linear state feedback, and convergence to a desirable bounded steady regime is automatically achieved by the optimal closed-loop dynamics. The free–endpoint problem already pro-duces trajectories that converge to an equilibrium, so a fixed–endpoint requirement at infinity is implicitly satis-fied and does not need to be imposed as an additional constraint. If eℓsq is nonnegative but not strictly positive in x, some directions in state space are not directly penalized, and the minimizing input for the free–endpoint problem need not be stabilizing. In this semidefinite case, the usual detectability condition for the pair ( eQ1/2, eA), which might depend on the weights selection too, ensures that any unpenalized mode in ker( eQ1/2) is at least asymptotically stable, so that the free–endpoint optimal solution is again stabilizing and trajectories converge to an equilibrium. Notice that, since Proposition 2 guarantees that Ac is Hurwitz, for sufficiently small engagement weights WEN 

and/or sufficiently large weights penalizing overexposure (entering R), eA = Ac −R−1N is a small perturbation of Ac

and remains Hurwitz (Horn and Johnson, 2012, Ch. VI). Therefore, whenever the relaxed spectral inequalities of Lemma 3 and Corollaries 4–5 hold and thus eQ ⪰ 0, sufficiently small weights in WEN imply eA Hurwitz and thus detectability of ( eQ1/2, eA), so the semidefinite case is also benign. The situation changes qualitatively when eQ has both posi-tive and negative eigenvalues, so that eℓsq is sign-indefinite. In this regime, the unconstrained infinite-horizon prob-lem may fail to admit an optimal input both in the fixed-endpoint and free endpoint cases (Trentelman, 1989, Thm.s 4.1 - 5.1). Even when minimizers exist, they need not be stabilizing: for the free–endpoint problem, it can be optimal, from the viewpoint of the raw cost functional, to exploit directions where the quadratic form is negative or zero and to generate large trajectories, rather than converging to a desirable equilibrium. To recover a mean-ingful fixed–endpoint behavior in this indefinite setting, one typically restricts attention to stabilizing solutions and appeals to the generalized Riccati-equation framework for indefinite LQ problems (see, e.g., Trentelman (1989)). This, however, amounts to solving the optimal control problem (7) with the additional constraint of closed-loop stability, which in general no longer coincides with the un-constrained minimization of eJ in (7). From the viewpoint of recommendation-system design, this is conceptually un-satisfactory: it means that the weight selection in the cost and the actual behavior of the closed-loop trajectories are misaligned, and stability (or convergence to a fixed endpoint) is enforced externally rather than emerging from the performance index itself. For this reason, in the present work we interpret the conditions of Lemma 3 and Corollaries 4–5 as design con-straints on the weights ( WD, W P, W EN , W EX , α F), ensuring that the effective quadratic form eℓsq is sign-definite (or at least semidefinite together with detectability of ( eQ1/2, eA)). This prevents the problem from entering the indefinite regime, in which one must impose stability as an addi-tional constraint, and provides a principled justification for focusing on weight configurations that satisfy the positive-definiteness conditions while still capturing the trade-off between engagement, polarization, deviation from base-line, and exposure regularization. In the following section, we highlight the possible pitfalls of the free–endpoint LQ framework in the context of sign semidefiniteness and indefiniteness of (9) via a set of examples, further motivating the use of Lemma 3 and Corollaries 4–5 as design constraints on the weights. 5. The three examples in this section illustrate complemen-tary failure modes that arise when the weights in the per-formance index are chosen outside the cone characterized by Lemma 3 and Corollaries 4–5. In the first example, the effective stage cost is sign-indefinite, but the free–endpoint LQ problem still admits a unique optimal feedback which leaves an unstable mode unregulated. Hence, the closed loop fails to be stable, despite having opinions and recom-mendations that minimize the cost. This contradicts our design goal of avoiding polarization and ensuring align-ment. In the second example, the stage cost is again sign-indefinite. The algebraic Riccati equation admits extremal solutions, but the condition ker( P+ − P−) ⊂ ker( P−)is violated: the value of the optimal control problem is finite, yet no optimal input exists. The infimum is not attained from some (possibly nontrivial) set of initial pos-sible opinion. Finally, in the third example, the selection of weight in the performance index is such that the stage cost is positive semidefinite, but the couple ( eQ1/2, eA) is not detectable. The consequence is that the free–endpoint optimum coincides with zero input, the recommendation system does not counteract the unstable opinion dynamics, and the state diverges even though the control effort is minimized, again contradicting the design goals. Taken together, these examples show how, in practice, over-weighting engagement relative to polarization, alignment, and effort can make the infinite-horizon problem ill-posed (nonattainment) or produce optimal policies that are not aligned with the very design goals encoded in the perfor-mance index. 6. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION We identify the main contributions of our work in the use of a combination of explicit opinion dynamics modeling, together with closed-loop optimal control, and the deriva-tion of explicit spectral conditions for the well-posedness of recommendation systems. Our analysis and examples aim to promote the idea that the spectral conditions given in Lemma 3 and Corollar-ies 4–5 should be interpreted as design constraints on the performance index’s weights selection that ties the engagement weights to the penalization required for po-larization, deviation from baseline, and control effort. If engagement is over-weighted relative to these regularizing terms, the effective quadratic form becomes sign-indefinite or semidefininte, pushing the design into the free–endpoint LQ regime in which closed-loop stability is no longer guar-anteed by the performance index itself. In that regime, stability has to be enforced as an additional constraint, which does not fit the idea that the cost should encode the desired interaction between users and the platform. The present work has several limitations that point to future directions of work. We assumed full-state feedback and a time-invariant, known interaction structure, encoded in L, C and Aa, which is homogeneous across agents and topics. In practice, the social graph and inter-topic logic should be estimated from data, and can be time-varying and uncertain. Extending the analysis to partial observability and controllability, output feedback, model uncertainty, and to the context in which the model param-eters have to be learned recursively, is therefore a natural next step. Likewise, we considered quadratic penalties, a static state feedback and unconstrained inputs: incorporating further constraints on exposure, fairness, or safety, or rewarding variety on the content proposed (hence requiring dynamic feedback), as well as constraints on the pool of possible rec-ommendations input (e.g., via saturation or quantization, and constraints on the input matrix), would better reflect real-world platforms but leads to constrained or nonlinear optimal control problems. Finally, while our performance index weights can be het-erogeneous across users and topics, in this paper they are treated as fixed design and modeling parameters rather than learned or adapted. Embedding the pro-posed spectral guardrails into learning-based pipelines, so that engagement-oriented objectives are tuned subject to stability and polarization constraints would connect the present model-based approach with data-driven recom-mendation architectures (Chandrasekaran et al., 2025). Furthermore, exploring distributed or graph-structured feedback laws that exploit sparsity and structure embed-ded in L and C would improve scalability to large net-works, which is a key principle when designing recommen-dation systems.
