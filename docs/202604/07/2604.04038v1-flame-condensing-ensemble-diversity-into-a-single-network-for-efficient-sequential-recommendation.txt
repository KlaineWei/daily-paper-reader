## Abstract 

Sequential recommendation requires capturing diverse user behav-iors, which a single network often fails to capture. While ensemble methods mitigate this by leveraging multiple networks, training them all from scratch leads to high computational cost and in-stability from noisy mutual supervision. We propose Frozen and 

Learnable networks with Aligned Modular Ensemble ( FLAME ), a novel framework that condenses ensemble-level diversity into a single network for efficient sequential recommendation. During training, FLAME simulates exponential diversity using only two networks via modular ensemble . By decomposing each network into sub-modules (e.g., layers or blocks) and dynamically combining them, FLAME generates a rich space of diverse representation pat-terns. To stabilize this process, we pretrain and freeze one network to serve as a semantic anchor and employ guided mutual learning .This aligns the diverse representations into the space of the remain-ing learnable network, ensuring robust optimization. Consequently, at inference, FLAME utilizes only the learnable network, achieving ensemble-level performance with zero overhead compared to a sin-gle network. Experiments on six datasets show that FLAME outper-forms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving up to 7.69 × faster conver-gence and 9.70% improvement in NDCG@20. We provide the source code of FLAME at https://github.com/woo-joo/FLAME_SIGIR26.

## 1 Introduction 

Sequential recommendation presents non-trivial challenges due to the diversity of user behaviors, the complexity of item spaces, and the multi-faceted nature of user–item interactions [ 4, 18 , 20 , 40 , 42 ,43 , 45 , 50 , 52 ]. Furthermore, a single user sequence can reflect vari-ous patterns—such as short-term interests, long-term preferences, or shifts in user tastes—making it difficult for a single model to cap-ture all relevant signals [ 30 , 37 , 39 , 45 ]. These challenges highlight the need for models that can interpret sequences from multiple perspectives. Instead of relying on a single model to capture the full range of behavioral patterns, a promising direction is to combine multiple models, each focusing on complementary aspects of the data. This is the core idea behind ensemble methods [ 1 , 11 , 13 ], which have shown strong performance in various domains by cap-turing diverse and complementary patterns. In this light, ensemble learning is particularly well-suited for sequential recommendation, where user intent and item semantics vary widely. Recently, several ensemble-based methods have been proposed to capture the diverse dynamics embedded within user sequences [ 11 , 12 , 54 ]. These approaches commonly train multiple networks from scratch in parallel, with each network encouraged to specialize in complementary behavioral patterns. Inspired by recent advance-ments in computer vision field [ 41 , 55 , 60 ], many of these methods [ 11 , 54 ] further incorporate mutual knowledge transfer among net-works to enhance collective learning. For example, EMKD [ 11 ]performs both representation-level contrastive learning and logit-level knowledge distillation, allowing individual networks to align their outputs while maintaining diversity in internal representa-tions. Such strategies aim to promote inter-network agreement and

Despite their effectiveness, recent ensemble-based methods suf-fer from two major limitations: computational inefficiency and train-ing instability . First, employing multiple networks significantly increases computational costs, including parameters, memory foot-print, and training time. In particular, typical ensemble prediction requires forwarding each network independently and aggregat-ing their output, resulting in training and inference costs that are several times higher than those of a single network. This makes such methods impractical for real-time services. Second, training all networks from scratch with mutual knowledge transfer often leads to unstable optimization, especially in the early stages when none of the networks has reliable prior knowledge [ 25 , 51 ], ultimately limiting performance. This problem can be substantially aggravated in sequential recommendation, where data tends to be sparse and noisy [ 16 ]. In our experiments, existing ensemble methods show large training fluctuations and require 2.06 to 2.94 times longer to converge than standard model (see Section 5.6). To address the inefficiency and instability of ensemble-based sequential recommendation methods, we focus on two key insights informed by previous literature and our empirical study. First, differ-ent sub-modules within a single network can capture distinct patterns .Prior works [ 10 , 22 , 46 ] commonly observe that as layers deepen, neural representations become increasingly abstract and discrimi-native. A similar phenomenon can be expected in sequential recom-mendation, where, for instance, the embedding module captures static item semantics, while early layers of the sequence encoder reflect localized and item-specific signals, and deeper layers en-code temporally smoothed representations of user preferences. This structural separation suggests that even a single network can inter-nally generate diverse representations by interpreting sequences from multiple perspectives. Building on this, our key idea is to sim-ulate ensemble-like behavior by leveraging an exponential number of sub-module combinations constructed from just two networks– without explicitly constructing multiple models. Second, pretraining and freezing just one network of an ensem-ble can substantially improve training stability . We empirically ob-serve that even a single pretrained network—trained on the same data and in the same manner, except without mutual knowledge transfer—can provide effective prior knowledge that guides the training of the remaining learnable networks, resulting in more stable and effective optimization. In Figure 1, we conduct experi-ments with SASRec [ 20 ] under three settings: Single (a single SAS-Rec), Ensemble-Scratch (two SASRecs trained from scratch with mutual knowledge transfer 1), and Ensemble-Guide (one network in Ensemble-Scratch is pretrained and frozen). Both networks in Ensemble-Scratch exhibit higher fluctuations and slower conver-gence than Single, whereas the learnable network in Ensemble-Guide converges more quickly and stably. Notably, while each network in Ensemble-Scratch underperforms compared to Sin-gle—indicating that noisy supervision between untrained models can hinder ensemble learning—the learnable network in Ensemble-Guide outperforms Single by leveraging guidance from the frozen network. This finding aligns with observations in self-distillation, where a pretrained teacher improves both training stability and final performance of an identical student [15, 33, 34]. Building on these insights, we propose Frozen and Learnable networks with Aligned Modular Ensemble ( FLAME ), a novel frame-work that condenses ensemble-level diversity into a single network for efficient sequential recommendation. FLAME consists of two networks with identical architecture and capacity: one is pretrained and frozen, and the other is learned from scratch. Each network comprises 𝑀 sub-modules, and each input is forwarded through a combination of frozen and learnable sub-modules–yielding 2𝑀 

unique representations. These representations are aligned into a unified semantic space via contrastive learning, utilizing the frozen network as a stable semantic anchor. Remarkably, this design allows FLAME to simulate the diversity of ensembling an exponentially large number of networks using just two, enabling the learnable network to capture complementary patterns without instability. Consequently, inference relies solely on the learnable network alone, achieving ensemble-level performance with the efficiency of a single network. Thus, FLAME presents a practical and robust alternative to conventional ensemble methods. Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We provide both conceptual and empirical evidence revealing the inefficiency and instability of conventional ensemble. 

• We propose FLAME, a framework that condenses ensemble-level diversity into a single network, achieving robust training stability and zero-overhead inference efficiency. 

• We conduct extensive experiments on six real-world datasets, where FLAME consistently outperforms state-of-the-art base-lines. Our results show that FLAME achieves superior accuracy with significantly lower training cost and inference latency.

## 3.1 Modular Ensemble 

Modular ensemble, depicted in Figure 2b, addresses the computa-tional inefficiency of conventional ensemble methods while preserv-ing, or even enhancing, representational diversity. Unlike conven-tional ensemble, which generates representations by forwarding the input through an entire network as a whole, modular ensemble flexibly chooses the sub-module to use at each stage from any of the networks. This design allows for a combinatorial expansion of representation paths without increasing the number of complete networks. For example, with three networks each split into three sub-modules, the modular ensemble produces 33 = 27 unique repre-sentations, compared to only 3 in a conventional setup. Conversely, even a small number of networks (e.g., two networks with three sub-modules each) can simulate the effect of an 8-network conven-tional ensemble through 23 = 8 modular combinations, significantly improving efficiency. 

## 3.2 Guided Mutual Learning 

Another critical challenge of conventional ensemble training, as ob-served in Figure 1, is optimization instability, presumably stemming from noisy supervision exchanged among randomly initialized net-works. This issue can be alleviated by pretraining and freezing some of the networks in ensemble. The frozen networks, imbued with prior knowledge, serves as a stable reference during training, guiding the rest learnable networks toward faster and more reliable convergence. In addition to stabilizing, this setup also improves the standalone quality of the learnable networks through mutual enhancement between networks [ 23 , 51 , 55 ]. This strategy inte-grates particularly well with modular ensemble, where fine-grained knowledge exchange occurs structurally at the sub-module level. 

## 3.3 Empirical Evidence 

To motivate the integration of the two core designs into FLAME framework, we empirically examine their effectiveness in the con-text of ensemble learning. For an ensemble to be effective, it must satisfy two critical conditions: diversity among representations and 

reliability of each individual network [ 9, 58 ]. From this perspective, we evaluate how the introduced designs–modular ensemble and guided mutual learning–are specifically tailored to achieve diversity and reliability, respectively, and thus serve as a solid foundation for an ensemble recommender. 

Diversity. Figure 3a visualizes the distribution of sequence rep-resentations generated by two distinct SASRec-based ensemble strategies: 1) a 2-network conventional ensemble and 2) a 2-network modular ensemble, where each network is decomposed into two sub-modules. While both methods produce separated representa-tions, modular ensemble doubles the output diversity (four cluster types versus two) for the same number of networks. Specifically, because different sub-modules within a single network capture distinct patterns, their combinations enable the creation of an ex-ponentially diverse set of representations. Consequently, modular ensemble expands its representational coverage to include the di-verse user behaviors (i.e., the yellow and green clusters) entirely missed by conventional ensemble. This capacity for capturing the multifaceted nature of user interests is critical for advancing se-quential recommendation performance. 

Reliability. Figure 3b presents the recommendation perfor-mance of individual network in Ensemble-Scratch and Ensemble-Guide in Figure 1. The learnable network in Ensemble-Guide achieves the highest performance among all four networks, indicating the guidance from the frozen network during training enhances the reliability of individual network. It is also noteworthy that the two networks in Ensemble-Scratch both underperform compared to the frozen network in Ensemble-Guide (or equivalently, a single SAS-Rec), suggesting that noisy mutual supervision among networks in the early stages of training can lead to sub-optimal convergence. 

Put it together. To validate the synergy between modular en-semble and guided mutual learning, we compare our proposed FLAME against a 4-network conventional ensemble, both built upon SASRec backbone. Both methods produce four representa-tions, and we evaluate their diversity and reliability via the pairwise exclusive-hit ratio (PER) [ 19 ], as shown in Figure 3c,d. ## 4 Proposed Framework: FLAME 

We introduce Frozen and Learnable networks with Aligned Modular 

Ensemble for sequential recommendation, named FLAME , a unified framework that combines modular ensemble with guided mutual learning. FLAME utilizes only two networks, yet efficiently achieves representation diversity through modular ensemble. Among them, one network is pretrained and frozen, which serves to enhance the reliability of the remaining learnable network through guided mutual learning. FLAME performs accurate and efficient recom-mendation using only the learnable network at inference time. The overall architecture of FLAME is illustrated in Figure 4.

For DHEN, which ensembles two heterogeneous networks from three architectural options (i.e., self-attention, MLP and CNN), we em-pirically find that the self-attention and MLP pairing is the most effective combination. For FamouSRec, the model integrates four heterogeneous architectures, including self-attention, GRU, MLP and Mamba-based networks. ## 6 Conclusion 

We present FLAME, a novel framework achieving the benefits of ensemble learning–diversity and reliability–without the associ-ated high costs. FLAME utilizes just two networks, one frozen and one learnable, to efficiently generate diverse representations via sub-module combinations, while the frozen network provides stable guidance. Crucially, this design condenses ensemble power into a single learnable network, ensuring zero-overhead inference suitable for real-time applications. Consequently, FLAME trains faster and more stably while achieving stronger performance, sug-gesting that scalable and effective ensemble learning is attainable without typical trade-offs. Future work could investigate elastic inference mechanisms that dynamically activate ensemble paths for high-uncertainty users to further optimize the accuracy-efficiency trade-off.
