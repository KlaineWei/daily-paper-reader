# Abstract 

Guava cultivation is considerably influenced by foliar and fruit diseases whose overlapping symptoms and environmental variability make accurate field-level diagnosis challenging. Numerous studies have been conducted to find efficient methods of diagnosing plant diseases, but most focus on image-level classification and do not include lesion localization or pixel-level segmentation of the images within a single framework of analysis. This study proposes a comprehensive framework for utilizing automated image analysis to classify guava leaf and fruit diseases at the image level, locate lesions, and segment lesions at the pixel level from multiple images of the same type of disease collected from various growing conditions. The dataset was enriched through three augmentation strategies including standard preprocessing, structured augmentation, and GAN-based synthetic image generation, expanding the effective training data to approximately 7,000 images, while a 5-fold cross-validation strategy guided model selection and final performance was assessed on a held-out test set. The experimental evaluation of multiple state-of-the-art Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for the classification of guava leaf and fruit diseases indicated that the model generated using the ResNet50+DenseNet121 model fusion achieved the highest classification accuracy of 98.20%. For lesion detection and segmentation, YOLOv8-seg outperformed Mask R-CNN, achieving mAP@0.5 of 0.907 and 0.889, and mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.783 and 0.769 for detection and segmentation, respectively, with a balanced precision–recall profile. The techniques of Explainable AI (XAI) were used to increase the transparency of this model by identifying areas in the image that are significant to the actual lesion. The framework was further designed with practical web-based deployment in mind, evaluating both lightweight and high-capacity models to balance computational efficiency against predictive accuracy. From this research, it was concluded that using model fusion, data augmentation, and segmentation-aware lesion detection would provide a solution for managing guava diseases effectively. # 1. Introduction 1

The guava ( Psidium guajava ) is a widely grown plant throughout the tropics and subtropical regions. Due to 2

its nutritional value and ability to adapt to a wide variety of growing environments, it is also commonly 3

referred to as “Queen of Fruits” [1]. Guavas are a good source of potassium, dietary fiber, antioxidants, 4

vitamin C, and support both immune system function and cardiovascular health [2]. Guava is also an 5

important part of the fruit production system in developing countries because of its ability to adapt to many 6

different climates [3], and it has a strong economic importance in these areas because of its contributions to 7

local economies. 8

Even with the economic importance of guava, production is susceptible to numerous illnesses induced by 9

foliar and fruit fungal, bacterial, and viral pathogens [3]. These diseases usually lead to decreased yield 10 

quality, increased production costs, and increased dependence on chemical treatments. Disease outbreak 11 

costs have the potential to be very high in major guava-producing areas, such as Bangladesh, where guava is 12 

produced on a total of approximately 0.3 million hectares per year with a total production of over 3.7 million 13 

metric tons [4]. In addition, many factors, such as variations in cultivar type, growth stage, and 14 

environmental conditions, frequently result in similar visual symptoms, making reliable field-level diagnosis 15 

challenging. 16 

Farmers and agricultural specialists typically use only visual assessment methods to diagnose diseases. 17 

Nonetheless, such evaluations are intrinsically subjective and may exhibit inconsistency, especially when 18 

symptom patterns overlap or vary with illumination and humidity conditions [5]. While there are reliable 19 

diagnostic tests, like those that utilize real-time polymerase chain reaction and mass spectrometry, they 20 

require expensive equipment and a person with technical expertise to operate [6]. Therefore, making the use 21 

of these systems impractical for large-scale disease identification efforts. These limitations have resulted in an 22 

increased interest in automated imaging systems for disease diagnosis using image-based plant analysis. 23 

The advancement of machine learning and deep learning technology has produced automated analysis of 24 

plant disease with considerable results [6]. Research into the use of convolutional neural networks (CNN) for 25 

the classification of guava leaf diseases has reported both high rates of accuracy. As an example, the 26 

Inception-ResNet50 method achieved an accuracy of 97.50% [7], while there is also a reported accuracy of 27 

97.32% using a multi-fusion meta-learning approach [8]. Additionally, the combination of EfficientNetV2 and 28 

Vision Transformers yielded an accuracy of approximately 95%, with the addition of interpretability via 29 

Grad-CAM [9]. Similarly, DenseNet201 has been reported to achieve an accuracy of 96% for classifying the 30 

fruit disease [10]. Other types of segmentation-based models (GLSM, YOLOv5, and GIP-MU-Net), which 31 

are aimed at localizing infected areas more precisely, have also been reported to have been explored beyond 32 

that of image classification [5]. 33 

Despite the above-mentioned studies involving the classification of guava leaf diseases using CNN and 34 

segmentation-based models, the vast majority of studies have employed only image-level prediction methods 35 

and do not combine lesion localization and pixel-level segmentation methods into a single framework [5 –10]. 36 

As such, these existing methods are limited in their applicability for performing quantitative severity 37 

estimation and implementing targeted intervention. Furthermore, the vast majority of studies employed a 38 

limited number of images, as well as imbalanced datasets. There is little consistency in the use of 39 

cross-validation, nor has the interpretability of models been given the same importance as the physical 40 

attributes of models when designing and conducting research. Additionally, there are very few studies that 41 

evaluate detection and segmentation models in the same annotation and training conditions as it pertains to 42 

either of these methods; thus, fairness and accuracy of comparison are extremely difficult to achieve. 43 

These considerations strongly imply that a single, segmentation-aware and interpretable framework is 44 

needed to balance both the rigor of the methodology as well as the practicality of the application for guava 45 

disease management. 46 

The contributions of the current study are listed below: 47 

1. Development of a standardized deep learning framework that includes the three parts of classification, 48 

localization of the lesion, and pixel-level segmentation under a unified evaluation system. 49 

2. Implementation of a systematic approach to enhance the data for achieving better generalized models 50 

across different agricultural field environments. 51

# 2. Literature Review 59 

Recent advancements in machine learning and deep learning techniques have dramatically improved 60 

automated detection of plant diseases through image analysis. Different convolutional neural network (CNN) 61 

architectures and segmentation models have been employed for diagnosing and detecting diseases on fruit 62 

and leaf samples. Several studies on classifying and detecting guava diseases have been conducted. Other 63 

fields of research on plant disease detection have resulted in the development of new CNN architectures and 64 

learning methods. This section indicates a large number of studies relevant to the proposed method and 65 

highlights where limitations exist, which necessitate the need for the proposed method. 66 

A hybrid architecture combining GIP-MU-Net for infected patch segmentation, GLSM for leaf region 67 

segmentation, and a YOLOv5-based module for multi-disease detection from a single leaf was suggested by 68 

Rashid et al. [5]. A total of 2,542 guava leaf images were used in the dataset during evaluation. There were 69 

five disease categories or groups to classify these images. The accuracy of the segmentation stage of the 70 

framework was 92.41%. The detection stage of the framework achieved the following: 73.3% precision; 73.1% 71 

recall, and 71% mAP@0.5. Comparatively, the segmentation stage achieved better performance results than 72 

the detection stage due to using stricter IoU thresholds during segmentation than detection. Other research 73 

has focused on feasibility from the end-user perspective instead of multi-component frameworks, such as the 74 

device-friendly framework proposed by Nandi et al. [6]. In that work, quantization (float16, dynamic range 75 

quantization, etc.) was applied to decrease model size while maintaining comparable levels of accuracy for 76 

guava fruit and leaf diseases using a dataset of 1,834 images, achieving 94.93% classification accuracy. Guler 77 

et al. [7] explored ensemble and fusion methods for classifying guava diseases through an evaluation of deep 78 

networks under hybrid augmentation using 2,063 guava leaf images, where the InceptionV3–ResNet50 79 

ensemble achieved 97.50% accuracy with an F1-score of 0.975 and AUC of 0.9934. In a related vein, Asim et 80 

al. [8] applied an ensemble machine-learning approach with multi-fusion metacognition on 560 guava leaf 81 

images across seven disease categories, obtaining 97.32% classification accuracy, which supports the idea that 82 

using feature-level fusion together with meta-learning can be useful for limited datasets. 83 

In addition to building reliable models that accurately classify objects, an increasing number of recent 84 

research efforts consider additional factors such as explainability and deployment of results. Farooqui et 85 

al. [9] used a hybrid EfficientNetV2–Vision Transformer model, applied the Grad-CAM algorithm for 86 

highlighting disease-mediated areas of tissue, and created an online portal for users to access detection results 87 

and explanations; their model achieved 95% classification accuracy across five guava leaf disease classes. 88 

Researchers have explored guava fruits for an array of disease identification, but studies can also serve to 89 

inform fine-grained classification tasks. Toptas et al. [10] set out to classify guavas commercially using 2,310 90 

fruit images representing nine classes, studying several varieties and ripening states while evaluating several 91 

pretrained CNN models through both stratified k-fold cross-validation and an 80:20 random train/test split. 92 

Comparison of the metrics obtained from models based on the architectural backbone of DenseNet201 showed 93 

the best performance with approximately 96.5% accuracy. In addition to using deep learning to conduct 94 

analysis of guavas, research has also employed classical machine learning approaches to analyze guava 95 

diseases within constrained data domains. For example, Oya et al. [11] applied preprocessing steps such as 96 

segmentation, contrast enhancement (CLAHE), and augmentation prior to training their models using 473 97 

guava fruit images, where SVM and ANN classifiers achieved close to 99% accuracy. Their findings suggest 98 

that pipeline design and preprocessing steps can significantly impact results achieved within any model class. 99

## 3.3 Annotation for Detection and Segmentation 226 

All annotations were manually verified to ensure consistency of lesion boundaries across disease classes. The 227 

same annotated dataset was used for both YOLOv8-seg and Mask R-CNN to ensure fair comparison under 228 

identical labeling conditions. Bounding boxes were used to mark the diseased and healthy regions, while 229 

polygon annotations were used to mark the boundaries of the lesions. These processes were done at the pixel 230 

level. The images were exported in YOLO format with normalized coordinates. Data augmentation 231 

techniques were used. These techniques were implemented using the Albumentations library. Flipping, 232 

rotation, scaling, and color changes were applied. These processes were done using the Ultralytics YOLO 233 

framework. 234 

## 3.4 Architecture of Applied Models for Classification 235 

This paper used a set of convolutional neural network (CNN) frameworks to detect guava leaf and disease in 236 

fruits: DenseNet121, NASNetMobile, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, Xception, EfficientDualBlock CNN, and a 237 

hybrid ResNet50+DenseNet121 model. The choice of these architectures was based on the proven 238 

effectiveness in the situation of plant disease recognition and the ability to identify salient visual patterns in 239 

agronomic imaging. Transfer learning was used, and the pretrained network weights were fine-tuned in order 240 

to boost the generalization performance in an environment with a relatively small set of data. 241 

3.4.1 DenseNet121 242 

DenseNet121 is one model that makes use of dense connectivity, that is, every layer obtains the feature maps 243 

of all preceding layers. This architecture allows effective sharing of features and improved gradient 244 

propagation, hence allowing the network to remember both the low-level and the high-level visual 245 

information. The features make DenseNet121 highly suitable for the classification of plant diseases using 246 

moderate-sized databases [45]. The dense connectivity mechanism is defined in Eq (1). 247 

zl = Fl([ x0, x 1, . . . , x l−1]) (1) Where, zl is the output of the lth layer and Fl(·) is a composite function that includes convolution, batch 248 

normalisation, and nonlinear activation, and [ x0, x 1, . . . , x l−1] is a concatenation of the feature maps 249 

produced by all previous layers. 250

3.4.2 EfficientDualBlock CNN 251 

Besides pretrained models, a lightweight customized model was developed, which was called 252 

EfficientDualBlock CNN, to explore the possibility of a computationally efficient solution to real-time disease 253 

classification. It is made up of several dual convolution blocks, which combine standard convolution and 254 

depthwise convolution, and then a concatenation of features and pooling layers. The network ends with 255 

Global Average Pooling, dense layers, dropout regularization, and a softmax classifier, thus making it 256 

possible to efficiently aggregate features with fairly low computational complexity [46]. The convolutional 257 

operation that is used in the dual block is mathematically expressed in Eq (2). 258 

Y = φ(K ∗ X + b) (2) Where, X is the input feature map, K represents convolution filters, b is the bias, ∗ denotes convolution, 259 

and φ is the activation function. 260 

3.4.3 InceptionV3 261 

Several commonly used CNN architectures were also compared and analyzed. InceptionV3 was chosen 262 

because it can extract multi-scale visual features using parallel convolutional filters in its inception modules. 263 

This setup allows the network to record disease symptoms with variability in size and texture, a common 264 

attribute of plant disease datasets [47]. The output of an Inception module is the resultant activation of the 265 

parallel convolutional branches of that module. As shown in Eq (3), features are extracted at different 266

## 4.4 Deployment and Web-Based Implementation 483 

In the following section, the process of developing the web-based decision support system, GuavaVision AI, is 484 

discussed. This system is specifically designed to help in the automated detection of diseases that occur in 485 

guava fruits as well as leaves. The system is designed such that the classification, detection, and 486 

segmentation techniques that are powered by deep learning can be performed together in one single pipeline, 487 

thus helping in the real-time diagnosis of diseases through the client-server architecture. 488 

4.4.1 System Architecture 489 

The GuavaVision AI system is based on a client-server architecture, which is built on a React-based client 490 

and a Flask server that provides a RESTful API and cross-origin resource sharing (CORS) for efficient 491 

communication. The inputs provided by the client in the form of guava images are Base64 encoded and sent 492 

over an HTTP request. The inputs are processed and converted into a final output by decoding the inputs, 493 

resizing them, normalizing them, and reconstructing them into RGB form. The unified inference pipeline is 494 

based on a fusion model (ResNet50+DenseNet121) for classification and YOLOv8-seg for detection, 495 

segmentation, and localization. The output of the inference is provided in a structured JSON format and is 496 

also shown to the client in the form of a canvas overlay. The architecture is based on TensorFlow and Keras 497 

for a GPU-enabled Flask server. The overall architecture is shown in Fig 20. 498

which helps in real-time interaction with the underlying deep learning models. 502 

The user interface, as represented in Fig 21(a), helps users in uploading images of guava leaves or fruits 503

# Conclusion 512 

This study presented a unified deep learning framework for the classification, detection, and pixel-level 513 

segmentation of guava leaf and fruit diseases. Among the evaluated classification models, the 514 

ResNet50+DenseNet121 fusion model achieved the best performance (98.20% accuracy), which showed that 515 

the fusion of residual learning with dense feature reuse has a significant impact on multi-class classification of 516 

plant diseases. DenseNet121 performed well independently as well, and the performance of MobileNetV2 was 517 

an efficient lightweight option that could be deployed to the web. 518 

To localize and segment diseases, YOLOv8-seg was found to perform better than Mask R-CNN with 519 

higher detection and segmentation accuracy (mAP@0.5 = 0.907 and 0.889) and faster inference, which makes 520 

it an appropriate choice in real-time agricultural monitoring. The interpretability of the models was 521 

confirmed through Grad-CAM, Saliency, SmoothGrad, LIME, and SHAP, and regularly demonstrated the 522 

presence of disease lesions and patterns of infections, which confirms biologically significant predictions. The 523 

experimental results demonstrate that integrating classification, detection, and segmentation within a unified 524 

framework improves both diagnostic accuracy and interpretability, making the system suitable for practical 525 

agricultural applications. 526 

Although the results were encouraging, the visual similarity between some of the diseases (e.g., 527 

Phytophthora and Styler and Root) as well as the relatively small sample size (527 images) can restrict the 528 

generalization. In the future, the direction of work involves increased field datasets, lightweight edge portable 529
