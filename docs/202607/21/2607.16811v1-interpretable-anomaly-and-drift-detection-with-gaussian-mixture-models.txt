# Interpretable Anomaly and Drift Detection with Gaussian Mixture Models 

Behnam Asadi 

behnam.asadi@gmail.com 

July 21, 2026 

Abstract 

We revisit Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) as a lightweight, interpretable 

tool for anomaly detection and, in particular, for detecting distributional drift in data streams. We make three practical choices explicit and evaluate them on seven public benchmarks. First, the number of mixture components is selected auto-matically by the Bayesian Information Criterion, initialised by k-means, remov-ing the need to fix it in advance. Second, individual observations are scored by their negative log-likelihood under a GMM fitted to normal data, with thresholds set at a target false-alarm rate using Extreme Value Theory. Third, the same inter-pretable model extends to distributional drift: each Gaussian component is a named “regime,” and the fraction of a stream window that matches no regime—its unex-plained mass —is a drift signal that is itself the explanation. We benchmark this against a model-free kernel two-sample test (Maximum Mean Discrepancy, MMD) and against two GMM-to-GMM divergences (a closed-form Cauchy–Schwarz di-vergence and a matching-based KL surrogate). Across seven benchmarks ranging from 3 to 64 dimensions and five random splits, the GMM point detector is com-petitive with—though rarely more accurate than—six baselines (Isolation Forest, LOF, one-class SVM, ECOD, COPOD and an autoencoder), while uniquely yield-ing an interpretable model. For drift, MMD is the strongest pure detector, but the interpretable unexplained-mass statistic matches it when anomalies form novel regimes (and honestly fails, as MMD does not, when drift is a pure re-weighting of existing regimes). Every alarm is explainable: anomalies lie a median of 3σ–10 σ

outside their nearest regime vs. ≈ 1σ for normal points, and a drift alarm reports the fraction of the window matching no known regime. All code and experiments are released.

Anomalies are patterns in data that do not conform to a well-defined notion of normal behaviour. Detecting them is critical across domains: intrusion detection for cyber-security, fraud detection in financial transactions, fault detection in safety-critical sys-tems, and condition monitoring of machines and robots [4]. Figure 1 illustrates the idea in two dimensions: most observations fall in the dense regions N1 and N2, whereas points o1, o2 and the small cluster O3 lie far from them and are flagged as anomalous. 

Figure 1: A simple example of anomalies in a two-dimensional data set: N1, N 2 are normal regions; o1, o 2 are point anomalies; O3 is a small anomalous cluster. This work originated in monitoring the joint-current streams of a six-legged walking robot, but the method is domain-agnostic and we evaluate it as such. We draw a dis-tinction that is often blurred in practice and that organises the whole approach: • Point anomalies: is an individual observation unlikely under normal behaviour? This is naturally answered by the likelihood a density model assigns to the point. • Distributional / drift anomalies: has the distribution of a recent window of the stream shifted away from normal behaviour? Individual points may each look normal while their collective distribution has changed—a regime change, sensor degradation, or a novel operating mode [8; 18]. This is naturally answered by a 

distance between distributions .Gaussian Mixture Models serve both tasks with a single, interpretable model: a weighted set of Gaussian components that approximates any continuous density to arbitrary ac-curacy [2; 20]. Unlike black-box detectors, a GMM exposes named “regimes” (its components), so an alarm can be attributed to which regime the data left and how far .

Contributions. (i) We present a compact, reproducible pipeline that couples auto-matic GMM model selection (BIC, k-means-initialised) with likelihood-based point scoring and Extreme-Value-Theory thresholding. (ii) For drift detection we introduce an interpretable “unexplained-mass” statistic on the same GMM—the fraction of a 2window matching no known regime—and benchmark it against a model-free MMD two-sample test and two GMM-to-GMM divergences—one closed-form (Cauchy–Schwarz, exact for mixtures) and one matching-based approximation (a KL surrogate). (iii) We give an honest empirical study on seven public benchmarks ( d ∈ [3 , 64] , five random splits) with six baselines (classic and modern) and an EVT threshold-calibration check, reporting where the GMM loses as well as where it wins. (iv) We separate two drift regimes— contamination by point-outliers vs. in-support re-weighting of regimes— and show which detectors each regime needs, exposing what the interpretable statistic can and cannot do. All code is released. 

# 2 Related Work 

Anomaly and novelty detection are surveyed in Chandola et al. [4] and Pimentel et al. [18]. GMMs have long been used for density-based anomaly detection [20]; the pa-rameters are estimated by Expectation–Maximization [6], and the number of compo-nents is commonly chosen by the Bayesian Information Criterion [23] or the Inte-grated Completed Likelihood [1]. Popular alternative detectors include Isolation For-est [16], the Local Outlier Factor [3], and the one-class SVM [22]; more recent tabular detectors include the parameter-free ECOD [15] and COPOD [14], and autoencoder reconstruction-error methods [21]. We benchmark against all of these. None of these, however, exposes an interpretable model of normality. Comparing two GMMs requires a distance between mixtures. The KL divergence, central to statistical pattern recognition [7], has no closed form between two Gaussian mixtures; practical work therefore uses approximations—Monte-Carlo, the variational and matching bounds of Hershey and Olsen [11], or the matching approximation of Goldberger et al. [9]. An alternative is to use a divergence that is closed-form for mixtures: Kampa et al. [13] derived such an expression for the Cauchy–Schwarz di-vergence, which we adopt. Distance-based comparison of GMMs has been used, e.g., for speaker and music similarity [12]. For streaming settings, Extreme Value Theory provides principled thresholds at a chosen false-alarm rate [5; 24], and drift is studied broadly in Gama et al. [8]. An orthogonal, model-free route to comparing two samples is the kernel Maximum Mean Discrepancy [10], which we discuss as an alternative in Section 5. 33 Method

Drift detectors compared. Table 5 compares four window-level statistics: the exact Cauchy–Schwarz (CS) divergence; a matching-based KL surrogate; the unexplained-mass statistic Uτ (Eq. 4, the interpretable GMM detector, i.e. the fraction of a window beyond 3σ of every reference regime); and, as a model-free reference, the kernel Max-imum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) two-sample test [10], which compares the window sample directly to a reference normal sample without fitting any mixture. Three find-ings stand out. First, the KL surrogate is unstable : it falls below chance ( 0.5) on Shuttle (0.29 ) and Pendigits ( 0.46 ), confirming that an approximate, asymmetric KL is a poor drift statistic. Second, MMD is the strongest pure detector , at or near AUC 1.0 on every dataset (lowest 0.93 on Satellite); the CS divergence is clearly weaker—never the best on any dataset—and collapses to chance on the 64 -dimensional Optdigits set (0.51 ). We therefore do not claim CS is a powerful detector; it is retained as a prin-cipled, closed-form ablation. Third, and most useful, the interpretable unexplained-mass statistic matches MMD in low-to-moderate dimensions (AUC 1.0 on KDD-http, KDD-smtp, Mammography and Shuttle; 0.87 on Pendigits) before it saturates in high dimensions ( 0.50 on Satellite and Optdigits). d-calibrated threshold would mitigate this. 4.6 Two kinds of drift: contamination vs. in-support re-weighting 

The experiment above injects point-outliers, so a window drifts by acquiring anomalies that lie outside the normal support. But we motivated drift more broadly (Section 1) as the case where individual points may each look normal while their collective distribu-tion has changed. These are genuinely different, and they need different detectors. To isolate the second case, we build drifted windows that contain only real normal points 

but drawn with re-weighted regime proportions (we assign each normal point to its nearest reference component and over-sample the dominant regimes). No point is an outlier—the “outlier fraction” column of Table 6 stays at the clean baseline—yet the distribution has genuinely shifted. The result is a clean dissociation. On the datasets where the re-weighting truly keeps every point in support (KDD-http, KDD-smtp and Mammography, outlier fraction ≤

2% ), the interpretable unexplained-mass statistic is blind : its AUC is at or below chance (0.47 , 0.17 , 0.01 ), because clean and drifted windows contain the same in-support points. The closed-form CS divergence is unreliable ( 0.26 –0.74 across datasets, strong on some, below chance on others). Only the model-free MMD test detects the re-weighting robustly (AUC 0.98 –1.0 on all seven). (On the higher-dimensional sets Satellite and Optdigits the outlier fraction is 1.0 not because re-weighting creates out-liers but because of the same fixed-τ = 3 σ saturation as Table 5—every normal point sits beyond 3σ—so the unexplained-mass AUC there is pinned at exactly 0.50 ; the three low-dimensional datasets, with outlier fraction ≤ 2% , isolate the in-support ef-fect cleanly.) This is an honest limitation, and it clarifies the division of labour: the unexplained-mass statistic detects novel-regime drift (contamination, new operating modes) and explains it ; detecting in-support drift (a shift in the mix of known regimes) 11 requires a distribution-level test such as MMD, which does not explain its alarms. # 5 Discussion 

Interpretability. The GMM’s advantage over black-box detectors is not raw accu-racy but explanation: each alarm can be attributed to the mixture component (regime) whose responsibility dropped or whose region the data vacated, together with the di-vergence magnitude. This matters in condition monitoring, where operators need a reason, not just a flag. 13 Dimensionality. Density estimation degrades as D grows; the GMM remains com-petitive up to D=36 (Satellite) here, but for higher dimensions we recommend a dimensionality-reduction front-end (PCA or an autoencoder) before the mixture, or a switch to a dif-ferent detector family. 

When to use what. Use likelihood scoring (3) for point anomalies and the diver-gence (8) for drift. A model-free kernel two-sample test such as MMD [10] solves the drift problem without fitting mixtures and is a strong alternative; the GMM route earns its place when interpretable regimes are wanted or the mixture assumption is reasonable. 

Limitations. Results use five random splits at a single contamination level; a fuller study would sweep contamination rates and add more high-dimensional benchmarks. The drift experiment assumes windows large enough to fit a GMM, trading latency for the ability to see distributional change. The interpretability case study is shown on one dataset for clarity, though the same attribution applies to any GMM. 

# 6 Conclusion 

We presented an interpretable GMM pipeline for anomaly and drift detection: BIC-selected components, likelihood-based point scoring with well-calibrated EVT thresh-olds (in low-to-moderate dimensions), and an interpretable “unexplained-mass” drift statistic on the same model. On seven public benchmarks the point detector is com-petitive with (though rarely the best of) standard baselines while degrading gracefully. For drift, a model-free MMD test is the strongest detector; the unexplained-mass statis-tic matches it when anomalies form novel regimes and—honestly—fails when drift is a pure re-weighting of existing regimes, a case MMD still catches. The closed-form Cauchy–Schwarz divergence is retained as a principled ablation but is empirically dom-inated. The method’s durable value is not raw accuracy—LOF is often more accurate— but that every alarm is explainable: which regimes the data matches or fails to match, and by how much. Future work will add streaming/online GMM updates, deep base-lines, and a dimensionality-reduction front-end for high-dimensional data.
